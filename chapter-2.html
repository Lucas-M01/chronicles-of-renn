THE CHRONICLES OF RENN · The Raven That Cast No Shadow

Chapter II — The Forgotten Path

By Lucas Montenegro

Some paths are forgotten because no one remembers where they lead. Others are forgotten because someone made certain they would never be found again.

The third bell had barely finished ringing when Baranion heard his father's footsteps.

They were quick, deliberate, and unmistakable.

“Baranion!”

The boy stood frozen in the darkness of the storage room.

The raven's silver eyes watched him from atop the crate.

“Baranion!”

“I'm here!”

The door swung open.

Elrohir stood in the doorway, holding a lantern in one hand and his sword in the other.

His gaze moved from Baranion to the extinguished lamp, then to the scattered objects on the floor.

“What happened?”

Baranion opened his mouth.

For a moment, he considered telling his father everything.

The injured raven.

The missing shadow.

The voice.

But before he could speak, a distant horn sounded from the western edge of the settlement.

Elrohir's expression changed.

“Are you hurt?”

“No.”

“Did something attack you?”

“No, but—”

“Then stay inside.”

“Father, there's something—”

“Baranion.”

His father's voice was sharper than usual.

“Whatever it is, it can wait until I return.”

Elrohir turned toward the doorway.

Then stopped.

For a brief moment, his eyes settled on the crate behind Baranion.

The raven remained perfectly still.

Elrohir frowned.

“Is that a bird?”

Baranion felt his stomach sink.

Before he could answer, Vhar landed heavily on the railing outside.

The enormous raven gave a harsh, urgent cry.

Elrohir looked toward his companion.

Whatever passed between them made his expression darken.

“We'll discuss this later.”

He disappeared down the stairs.

Baranion heard the front door close.

Then silence.

For several heartbeats, he stood motionless.

Finally, he turned toward the injured raven.

“You could have warned me.”

The bird blinked.

Baranion sighed.

“Right. Of course.”

He crossed the room and opened the shutters.

Outside, the settlement was changing.

Lanterns flared to life along the walkways. Guardians moved between the platforms, some carrying bows, others accompanied by beasts whose eyes glimmered in the darkness.

Far below, among the enormous roots, pale lights drifted between the trees.

Search parties.

Something had happened near the western border.

And Baranion was beginning to suspect that the creature sitting quietly in his storage room knew exactly what it was.

By morning, the entire settlement was talking.

A western concealment matrix had failed.

For nearly seven minutes, a narrow section of the forest's protective boundary had become visible to anyone approaching from outside.

That alone was alarming.

But something worse had happened.

A patrol of four guardians had disappeared.

Their final signal had come from a region known as the Hollow Vale, an old stretch of forest where few people ventured.

Three search parties had gone looking for them.

None had found a trace.

Baranion learned all this from Lyra, who arrived shortly after sunrise with a basket of bread and an expression that suggested she had not slept.

“Your father is still out there,” she said.

Baranion stopped chewing.

“What?”

“He joined the search before dawn.”

“Why didn't anyone tell me?”

“Because you were supposed to be sleeping.”

“I wasn't sleeping.”

“I can tell.”

She glanced at the dark circles beneath his eyes.

Then she looked toward the storage room.

“How's our mysterious friend?”

Baranion swallowed.

“There's something I need to show you.”

Lyra stood before the wall with her arms crossed.

Baranion had spent the last several minutes moving a candle around the raven, attempting to demonstrate the impossible.

The crate cast a shadow.

The bandages cast a shadow.

Even the loose feathers beside the bird cast shadows.

But the raven itself remained absent from the wall.

“I saw it yesterday,” Lyra said.

“I know.”

“Then why are we doing this again?”

“Because I wanted to make sure.”

“Make sure of what?”

“That we weren't imagining it.”

Lyra gave him a flat look.

Baranion lowered the candle.

“There's more.”

He told her about the previous night.

About the lamp.

About his shadow moving without him.

And about the voice.

Lyra listened without interrupting.

When he finished, she looked at the raven.

“Did it speak?”

“I don't think so.”

“You don't think so?”

“The voice didn't come from its beak.”

“Then where did it come from?”

Baranion hesitated.

“Everywhere.”

Lyra was silent for a moment.

Then she walked toward the door.

“Where are you going?”

“To find someone who understands things like this.”

“My father?”

“No.”

She turned back.

“Someone who won't immediately lock you in your room.”

Seris Aelwyn lived in a narrow house near the eastern archive.

Unlike most buildings in Fyor, hers had been constructed almost entirely from pale wood, with tall windows and shelves visible through the glass.

Books occupied nearly every available surface.

Books on the tables.

Books beneath the tables.

Books stacked beside the stairs.

There were even books arranged along the windowsills, apparently serving as supports for potted plants.

When Lyra knocked, something crashed inside.

A moment later, the door opened.

Seris appeared with a pencil tucked behind one pointed ear and a faint streak of ink across her cheek.

She looked at Lyra.

Then Baranion.

Then the bundle in his arms.

“No,” she said.

Baranion blinked.

“We haven't asked anything yet.”

“You're carrying something that you're trying to hide. Lyra looks worried, and you look guilty. Whatever it is, no.”

Lyra sighed.

“Seris.”

“No.”

“It's important.”

“That's what you said when you brought me that stone that supposedly whispered at night.”

“It did whisper.”

“It was full of beetles.”

“They were unusually loud beetles.”

Seris rubbed her forehead.

Then the bundle moved.

A black head emerged from beneath Baranion's cloak.

Two silver eyes looked directly at her.

Seris stopped.

Her expression changed.

“Where,” she asked slowly, “did you find that?”

The raven stood on a wooden table in Seris's study.

Its injured wing remained bandaged, though it seemed more alert than it had the previous day.

Seris had opened every curtain in the room.

Sunlight poured through the windows.

She placed a polished metal plate beside the bird, then adjusted its angle.

The raven's reflection appeared clearly.

She held a small mirror above it.

Again, the reflection was perfectly ordinary.

Then she moved a candle behind the bird.

No shadow.

Seris repeated the experiment three times.

Finally, she sat down.

“Well?” Baranion asked.

Seris stared at the raven.

“I don't know.”

Lyra raised an eyebrow.

“You don't know?”

“Would you prefer that I invent an explanation?”

“No.”

“Then I don't know.”

Baranion looked disappointed.

Seris noticed.

“That doesn't mean we can't learn something.”

She stood and approached a cabinet.

From inside, she removed a thin wooden tablet covered in intricate geometric markings.

She placed it on the table.

“What is that?” Baranion asked.

“A diagnostic matrix. A simple one.”

“What's it supposed to do?”

“Reveal disturbances in the space around an object.”

She paused.

“Among other things.”

Seris positioned the tablet beside the raven.

The markings glimmered faintly.

For a moment, nothing happened.

Then a thin line of light appeared across the wood.

It stretched toward the bird.

Stopped.

And bent sharply downward.

Seris leaned closer.

Another line appeared.

Then another.

All of them curved away from the raven, as though something beneath the table were pulling them.

Lyra stepped back.

“Is that supposed to happen?”

“No.”

The lines began to tremble.

Seris reached for the tablet.

Before her fingers touched it, the raven gave a sharp cry.

Every marking went dark.

A crack appeared across the wood.

The tablet split in two.

Silence filled the room.

Baranion stared at the broken pieces.

“Was that expensive?”

Seris closed her eyes.

“Very.”

“I'm sorry.”

“You should be.”

The raven lowered its head.

For a moment, Baranion thought it almost looked ashamed.

Seris examined the broken tablet.

Then she looked beneath the table.

Her expression became thoughtful.

“The lines weren't reacting to the bird.”

“What do you mean?” Lyra asked.

“They were being drawn toward something else.”

“Something beneath the table?”

“No.”

Seris looked at the raven again.

“Something that isn't here.”

They spent the next hour searching through old records.

Seris brought out several volumes containing descriptions of unusual magical beasts, failed experiments, and incidents involving damaged concealment matrices.

Most were useless.

One described a species of white moth that disappeared when exposed to moonlight.

Another concerned a serpent whose reflection moved several seconds behind its body.

Baranion found the second account particularly unsettling.

“Why would anyone keep something like that?”

Seris didn't look up.

“Because someone has to record what exists.”

“Even if it's dangerous?”

“Especially then.”

Lyra sat near the window, watching the raven.

The bird had become restless.

It kept turning toward the west.

Whenever Baranion moved away from the table, its eyes followed him.

Finally, Lyra spoke.

“It's trying to go somewhere.”

Seris glanced up.

“What?”

“The raven. Look.”

Baranion approached.

The bird raised its head.

Then it hopped toward the edge of the table.

Its injured wing shifted painfully.

“Easy,” Baranion murmured.

The raven stopped.

But its gaze remained fixed on the western window.

Seris frowned.

“The Hollow Vale lies in that direction.”

Baranion looked at her.

“That's where the patrol disappeared.”

“I know.”

The room fell silent.

Then Seris closed the book in front of her.

“I need to show you something.”

The eastern archive occupied three enormous chambers carved into the interior of an ancient tree.

Its walls were lined with shelves, and narrow bridges connected different levels of the collection.

The oldest records were kept in a restricted section beneath the main chamber.

Seris led them down a spiral staircase.

“Are we allowed to be here?” Baranion whispered.

“I am.”

“That wasn't my question.”

“Then perhaps you shouldn't ask it.”

Lyra smiled.

“I'm beginning to like her.”

Seris ignored them.

At the bottom of the stairs, she unlocked a heavy wooden door.

Inside were cabinets containing scrolls, stone fragments, and tablets wrapped in protective cloth.

She approached a shelf near the back.

“Several generations ago,” she began, “the concealment matrices protecting Fyor underwent a major reconstruction.”

“Why?” Lyra asked.

“Some of the older structures had become unstable. The records don't explain everything.”

She removed a narrow box.

“But they mention something unusual.”

Inside lay a bundle of brittle pages.

Seris unfolded one carefully.

A faded map appeared.

Baranion leaned closer.

It showed the western forest.

The familiar paths.

The boundary stones.

The Hollow Vale.

But there was something else.

A narrow trail extended beyond the vale, winding toward a cluster of symbols that had been crossed out.

“What is that?” Baranion asked.

Seris pointed to a line of writing beneath the map.

“The Forgotten Path.”

Lyra frowned.

“I've never heard of it.”

“Neither have most people.”

“Where does it lead?”

Seris hesitated.

“According to this record, to a place called the Stillroot Sanctuary.”

Baranion studied the faded symbols.

“Is it abandoned?”

“Apparently.”

“Why?”

Seris turned the page.

Most of the writing had been damaged by moisture.

Only a few sentences remained legible.

She read aloud.

“The bond was not severed. The vessel endured, though the keeper was lost.”

She paused.

Below that, another line had been written in a different hand.

“Do not answer the voice beneath the roots.”

Baranion felt cold.

Lyra noticed his expression.

“What?”

He swallowed.

“The voice.”

Seris looked at him.

“What voice?”

Baranion told her what he had heard in the storage room.

Find me.

Seris stared at the page.

For the first time since they had arrived, she seemed genuinely frightened.

They should have informed the guardians.

All three knew it.

But the western search parties had already been deployed, and Elrohir was somewhere beyond the boundary.

Seris argued that they needed to understand the old map before reporting anything.

Lyra argued that Baranion should remain in the settlement.

Baranion argued that the raven was the only creature that might know where the missing patrol had gone.

They argued for nearly twenty minutes.

In the end, they reached a compromise.

They would follow the marked trail only as far as the old western boundary stones.

They would not enter the Hollow Vale.

They would not approach the sanctuary.

And if they found anything dangerous, they would return immediately.

It was, Lyra declared, the sort of compromise that usually ended with everyone breaking their promises.

Baranion insisted that this time would be different.

Lyra did not look convinced.

The western forest was quieter than Baranion remembered.

The enormous trees grew close together, their branches forming a roof that blocked most of the sunlight.

Moss covered the roots.

Thin streams ran between stones.

Insects hummed beneath broad leaves.

But there were fewer birds than usual.

And no sign of the small animals that normally scurried across the forest floor.

Lyra noticed it first.

“Something's wrong.”

Baranion looked around.

“What?”

“The forest.”

Seris paused beside a boundary stone.

Its surface was covered in delicate markings.

Several had faded.

Others appeared distorted, as though the stone had been exposed to intense heat.

She touched one of the symbols.

A faint glow passed beneath her fingers.

Then vanished.

“This shouldn't be happening,” she murmured.

“Can you repair it?” Baranion asked.

“Not without the proper tools.”

She studied the stone.

“And not until I know what's causing the damage.”

The raven stirred beneath Baranion's cloak.

Its head emerged.

It looked toward the west.

Then gave a low croak.

Baranion followed its gaze.

Between the trees, almost hidden by hanging vines, stood another boundary stone.

But this one was different.

Its markings had been deliberately carved away.

Lyra approached it.

“Who would do that?”

Seris examined the damaged surface.

“Someone who wanted the path beyond it to disappear.”

Baranion looked ahead.

There was no visible trail.

Only roots, ferns, and fallen branches.

Then the raven gave another cry.

A breeze passed through the forest.

For a brief moment, the leaves on the ground shifted.

And beneath them, Baranion saw stones.

Small, flat stones arranged in a narrow line.

A path.

Hidden beneath decades of fallen leaves.

“The Forgotten Path,” he whispered.

They followed it.

At first, the trail was barely visible.

Then the trees began to change.

Their trunks became twisted.

Their roots rose from the earth in strange arches.

Some branches had grown together, forming natural tunnels.

The air grew colder.

Seris stopped several times to examine ancient markings carved into the bark.

“These aren't part of the current concealment system,” she said.

“Are they older?” Lyra asked.

“Much older.”

Baranion looked down at the raven.

The bird had become increasingly restless.

Its silver eyes remained fixed on the trail ahead.

After nearly an hour, they reached a clearing.

At its center stood the ruins of a small stone sanctuary.

Four pillars surrounded a circular platform.

Three had collapsed.

The fourth remained standing, covered in vines.

A shallow pool occupied the center of the platform.

Its water was perfectly still.

No insects moved across its surface.

No leaves floated upon it.

Even the breeze seemed to avoid the clearing.

Lyra drew her bow.

“We should leave.”

Baranion nodded.

For once, he agreed.

Then the raven began to struggle.

“Easy!”

The bird slipped from his cloak.

It fell to the ground, stumbled, and hopped toward the ruined platform.

“Wait!”

Baranion followed.

The raven stopped beside the pool.

Its silver eyes stared into the water.

Baranion approached slowly.

At first, he saw only his own reflection.

Then the raven stepped closer.

Its reflection appeared beside his.

But something was wrong.

In the water, the bird's injured wing was whole.

Its feathers were smooth.

And behind it stood a man.

Baranion spun around.

No one was there.

He looked back at the pool.

The man remained.

Tall.

Thin.

Dressed in robes that seemed to dissolve into darkness.

His face was obscured.

Not by a hood.

Not by shadow.

There was simply nothing where his features should have been.

Baranion stumbled backward.

“Lyra!”

The girl rushed toward him.

“What happened?”

“There's someone in the water!”

She looked.

The reflection was gone.

Only Baranion and the raven remained.

Seris knelt beside the pool.

Her expression tightened.

“Don't touch the water.”

“Why?” Baranion asked.

“Because I think this is where the old ritual took place.”

She pointed toward the stone platform.

Carved into its surface were several interlocking patterns.

Some resembled the matrices used in the Companion Ritual.

Others were unfamiliar.

And at the center lay a deep circular groove.

Seris brushed away the moss.

Beneath it, a line of writing emerged.

She read slowly.

“One life offered. One life preserved. One shadow left behind.”

Lyra looked at her.

“What does that mean?”

Seris did not answer immediately.

She examined the markings again.

“I don't know the complete method,” she admitted. “But these patterns suggest that someone attempted to bind two beings in a way that went far beyond the ordinary companion rites.”

“Who?” Baranion asked.

Seris shook her head.

“The name has been removed.”

She pointed to a damaged section of stone.

Someone had struck it repeatedly with a sharp tool.

Whatever name had once been written there was gone.

Baranion looked at the raven.

The bird stood beside the pool, trembling.

For the first time, it seemed afraid.

A branch snapped behind them.

Lyra turned instantly, raising her bow.

“Who's there?”

No answer.

Another branch broke.

Something moved between the trees.

Baranion reached for the short sword at his belt.

Seris stepped back from the platform.

Then a figure emerged from the forest.

A guardian.

His cloak was torn.

Blood stained one side of his face.

He stumbled into the clearing and collapsed to his knees.

Lyra lowered her bow.

“By the roots…”

Baranion rushed forward.

The man was breathing heavily.

His left arm hung uselessly at his side.

“Are you hurt?”

The guardian looked at him.

His eyes widened.

“Children?”

“We're from the settlement,” Lyra said. “What happened to you?”

The man tried to speak.

Only a hoarse whisper emerged.

Seris knelt beside him.

“Don't move. We'll help you.”

He grabbed her wrist.

His grip was surprisingly strong.

“Don't…”

His breathing quickened.

“Don't let it…”

He looked toward the sanctuary.

Then toward the raven.

The color drained from his face.

“No.”

Baranion followed his gaze.

The bird stood motionless beside the pool.

The guardian began to tremble.

“No, no, no…”

“What is it?” Lyra demanded.

The man stared at the raven.

“That thing…”

He swallowed.

“That thing was there.”

Baranion felt a chill.

“Where?”

The guardian's eyes filled with terror.

“When the others disappeared.”

They carried the injured guardian to the edge of the clearing.

Seris examined his wounds while Lyra prepared a signal arrow.

The man identified himself as Taren, one of the four guardians who had vanished the previous night.

He had escaped.

The others had not.

“What attacked you?” Lyra asked.

Taren shook his head.

“We weren't attacked.”

Baranion frowned.

“Then what happened?”

“We were following a disturbance near the boundary. The trees… the paths kept changing.”

He swallowed.

“We found the old stones. Then we heard someone calling.”

“Calling?” Seris repeated.

Taren nodded.

“A man's voice.”

Baranion's heart began to pound.

“What did it say?”

The guardian looked at him.

“It knew our names.”

Silence.

“We followed it,” Taren continued. “I told the others we should turn back, but the voice sounded like one of our own.”

He stared at the ground.

“Then we saw him.”

“Who?” Lyra asked.

Taren raised his head.

“A man standing between the trees.”

His voice trembled.

“He had no face.”

Baranion remembered the reflection in the pool.

Taren continued.

“He didn't walk toward us. He just stood there.”

“What happened next?” Seris asked.

“One of the others drew his sword.”

Taren closed his eyes.

“And his shadow stood up.”

Nobody spoke.

“The shadow rose from the ground,” he whispered. “Like a person pulling itself out of deep water.”

Baranion felt his stomach tighten.

“It grabbed him.”

Lyra slowly lowered her bow.

“What do you mean?”

“His own shadow grabbed him.”

Taren's breathing became ragged.

“He screamed. Then the others started running.”

He covered his face.

“I don't know where they went.”

Seris placed a hand on his shoulder.

“You're safe now.”

Taren shook his head violently.

“No.”

He looked toward the ruined sanctuary.

“No one is safe here.”

Lyra fired the signal arrow.

It rose above the trees and burst into a shower of pale green sparks.

A moment later, a distant horn answered.

The guardians had seen it.

Help was coming.

Baranion should have felt relieved.

Instead, he watched the raven.

The bird remained beside the pool.

Its silver eyes were fixed on the water.

And for the first time, Baranion noticed something that made his breath catch.

A shadow.

Not beneath the raven.

Not behind it.

But inside the pool.

A dark shape stretched across the still water, extending from the bird's reflection toward the center.

Baranion stepped closer.

The shadow moved.

Slowly.

Deliberately.

It reached toward him.

“Baranion!” Lyra shouted.

He stepped back.

The water rippled.

A voice rose from beneath the surface.

The same voice he had heard in the storage room.

“Find me.”

Seris turned sharply.

“You heard that?”

Baranion stared at her.

“You heard it too?”

She nodded.

The raven spread its injured wing.

For a moment, Baranion thought the bird was trying to fly.

Instead, it lowered its head toward the water.

And the voice spoke again.

This time, it sounded almost desperate.

“Please.”

The word hung in the clearing.

Then the water went dark.

Not cloudy.

Not muddy.

Black.

As though the pool had become a hole cut into the earth.

Seris seized Baranion's arm.

“Move!”

They retreated from the platform.

The raven remained behind.

“Come on!” Baranion called.

The bird did not move.

The darkness in the pool began to rise.

Slowly, a shape emerged.

First a hand.

Then an arm.

Then the outline of a man.

He rose without disturbing the water.

His robes hung motionless.

His face was smooth and empty.

No eyes.

No nose.

No mouth.

Yet Baranion knew he was being watched.

The figure turned toward the raven.

For several seconds, neither moved.

Then the faceless man raised one hand.

And the raven screamed.

Baranion had never heard a bird make a sound like that.

It was too deep.

Too human.

The raven collapsed beside the pool, its body convulsing.

Baranion moved before he could think.

“Stop!”

He rushed toward the platform.

Lyra shouted his name.

Seris tried to grab him.

But he was already moving.

The faceless figure turned.

Its raised hand shifted toward Baranion.

His shadow stretched across the stones.

Then stopped.

Baranion felt something seize his ankle.

He looked down.

His own shadow had wrapped itself around his leg.

He tried to pull free.

The darkness tightened.

A second hand rose from the stone.

Then a third.

Baranion stumbled.

His sword fell from his grasp.

The faceless man advanced.

Slowly.

Without a sound.

Baranion struggled, but the shadows held him fast.

Then an arrow struck the stone beside the figure.

Lyra.

“Get away from him!”

She fired again.

The arrow passed through the man's chest.

For an instant, his body scattered like smoke.

Then reformed.

Seris dropped to one knee and pressed both hands against the platform.

A pattern of pale lines spread across the stone.

The shadows holding Baranion trembled.

“Now!” she shouted.

He pulled.

The darkness loosened.

Baranion tore himself free and fell backward.

Lyra grabbed his collar and dragged him away.

Seris staggered to her feet.

The markings beneath her hands shattered into sparks.

The faceless man turned toward her.

And stopped.

From somewhere beyond the clearing came a deep, familiar cry.

Vhar.

A moment later, an enormous black shape swept between the trees.

The raven descended with astonishing speed, striking the faceless figure with both wings.

Dark feathers scattered through the air.

The figure dissolved.

Elrohir landed on the platform a heartbeat later.

His sword was already drawn.

“BARANION!”

The boy scrambled to his feet.

“Father!”

Elrohir positioned himself between his son and the pool.

Vhar circled overhead.

Several guardians emerged from the forest behind him.

The faceless figure began to reform.

Elrohir watched it carefully.

Then he noticed the injured raven.

Its silver eyes were fixed on the man rising from the water.

Something changed in Elrohir's expression.

Recognition.

Or perhaps fear.

“Everyone away from the pool,” he ordered.

The guardians obeyed.

Seris helped Taren to his feet.

Lyra pulled Baranion toward the trees.

But the boy resisted.

“The raven!”

Elrohir glanced at him.

“Leave it!”

“We can't!”

“Baranion!”

The faceless figure raised its hand again.

The shadows beneath the guardians began to writhe.

Elrohir moved.

His sword cut through the darkness with a sharp, sweeping motion.

Vhar descended beside him, his wings spreading wide.

For an instant, the shadows recoiled.

“GO!” Elrohir roared.

Lyra dragged Baranion toward the trees.

He struggled.

Then stopped.

Because the injured raven had turned its head.

Its silver eyes met his.

And Baranion heard something.

Not a voice this time.

A memory.

A sensation that did not belong to him.

Cold stone beneath bare feet.

The smell of rain.

A hand resting gently on black feathers.

And a man's voice, warm and tired.

Forgive me.

The vision vanished.

Baranion staggered.

Lyra caught him.

“What happened?”

He looked toward the pool.

The faceless figure stood motionless.

The injured raven lay at its feet.

And for the briefest instant, Baranion saw something impossible.

A shadow stretched from the raven's body.

Not toward the ground.

Not toward the trees.

But toward the faceless man.

As though the creature's missing shadow had been standing before them all along.

The guardians withdrew from the sanctuary.

Elrohir was the last to leave.

As he crossed the boundary stones, he pressed a small carved object into the earth.

The markings along its surface flared.

A pale barrier spread between the trees.

Beyond it, the ruined sanctuary vanished behind a curtain of shimmering air.

The faceless man did not follow.

Neither did the raven.

Baranion stood beside Lyra, breathing hard.

His father approached.

For a long moment, Elrohir said nothing.

Then he looked directly at his son.

“Tell me everything.”

Baranion did.

He told him about finding the injured raven.

About hiding it.

About the moving shadow.

The voice.

The old map.

And the sanctuary.

Elrohir listened without interruption.

When Baranion finished, his father closed his eyes.

“You should have come to me.”

“I know.”

“You could have died.”

“I know.”

“You put Lyra and Seris in danger.”

Baranion lowered his head.

“I know.”

Elrohir's voice softened.

“Then why?”

Baranion looked toward the shimmering barrier.

“Because it was hurt.”

His father said nothing.

“And because I think it's still hurt.”

Elrohir followed his gaze.

Baranion continued.

“That man… I don't think he's trying to kill the raven.”

“Then what do you think he's doing?”

The boy hesitated.

“I think he's trying to get something back.”

Elrohir frowned.

“What?”

Baranion looked at the place where the sanctuary had disappeared.

“His shadow.”

They returned to the settlement shortly before sunset.

Taren was taken to the healers.

The western boundary was placed under strict guard.

And the old records concerning the Stillroot Sanctuary were removed from the archive for examination.

Baranion expected to be confined to his room for the rest of his life.

Instead, Elrohir told him to remain at home until further notice.

It was, Baranion decided, only a slightly more polite version of the same punishment.

That evening, Lyra visited him.

She brought bread, fruit, and news.

“Seris found something.”

Baranion looked up.

“What?”

“A second record.”

“About the sanctuary?”

Lyra nodded.

“She thinks the man in the pool was once a guardian.”

Baranion sat upright.

“A guardian of Fyor?”

“Possibly. The records are incomplete.”

“Does she know his name?”

Lyra hesitated.

“Not yet.”

Baranion looked toward the empty crate in his storage room.

He had left the raven behind.

The thought made his chest ache.

“There's something else,” Lyra said.

He turned.

“When Seris examined the old records, she found a drawing.”

“Of what?”

Lyra reached into her bag.

She unfolded a small piece of paper.

It was a copy of an ancient illustration.

The drawing showed a man standing beside a raven.

The man's face had been damaged by age.

But the bird was unmistakable.

Black feathers.

Silver eyes.

And beneath its feet, a long, dark shadow.

Baranion stared at the image.

Lyra pointed to a line of writing beneath it.

“Seris translated this part.”

“What does it say?”

Lyra swallowed.

“The companion shall carry what the keeper can no longer bear.”

Baranion looked at her.

“What does that mean?”

“I don't know.”

She paused.

“But Seris thinks the raven didn't lose its shadow.”

Baranion slowly raised his eyes.

Lyra's voice dropped to a whisper.

“She thinks someone took it.”

Far away, beyond the western boundary, the ruined sanctuary stood beneath the darkening sky.

The pool was still.

The pillars were silent.

The forest around it seemed empty.

Beside the water, the injured raven lay motionless.

Then a hand emerged from the darkness.

Not the faceless man's hand.

A human hand.

Pale.

Scarred.

Trembling.

It reached toward the bird.

The raven opened its silver eyes.

A voice whispered from beneath the pool.

Not cold this time.

Not threatening.

Only exhausted.

“I'm sorry.”

The raven moved closer.

Its beak touched the outstretched fingers.

For a moment, the water reflected a man kneeling beside a great black bird.

His face was visible.

His eyes were filled with tears.

Then the reflection changed.

The man's features disappeared.

The hand withdrew.

And the voice returned, distant and hollow.

“Find me.”

Beneath the sanctuary, something ancient shifted in its sleep.

The raven closed its eyes.

And somewhere in the darkness, a shadow began to remember the shape of the man it had once belonged to.
