THE CHRONICLES OF RENN · The Raven That Cast No Shadow

Chapter I — The Shadowless Bird

By Lucas Montenegro

There are things that darkness conceals. And there are things that even shadows seek to escape.

The first sign that it was going to be a terrible day was the raven.

Not the raven itself, but what it dropped.

A small, smooth gray stone struck Baranion Finrod squarely on the forehead, waking him with enough force to make him sit bolt upright.

“Ouch!”

He rose so quickly that he hit his head against the wooden beam above his bed.

Outside the window, an enormous black raven watched the scene with its head tilted to one side. Its golden eyes seemed to gleam with satisfaction.

“I hate that bird,” Baranion muttered, rubbing his forehead.

The raven croaked.

“And you know it!”

Another croak.

“Don't pretend you don't understand!”

The bird spread its wings, revealing a wingspan nearly as wide as the window was tall, and disappeared among the branches.

Baranion had known that raven for as long as he could remember. Its name was Vhar, and it was his father's companion.

It also seemed to consider tormenting him every morning a personal duty.

“Baranion!” a voice called from the floor below. “If you're arguing with Vhar again, you should know he's not going to lose.”

“He started it!”

“He's a raven!”

“A very ill-mannered raven!”

Elrohir Finrod's laughter carried through the floorboards.

Baranion sighed.

The morning had barely begun.

The settlement of Fyor was slowly awakening.

Built among trees so ancient that their roots seemed to belong to another age, the settlement was a maze of walkways, platforms, and houses constructed around gigantic trunks.

Some homes stood so high that, on misty mornings, their inhabitants could look out their windows and see nothing but a white ocean beneath their feet.

Others lay hidden among thick branches, surrounded by hanging gardens, small vegetable plots, and lanterns of colored glass.

Wooden bridges and rope walkways connected the different sections of the settlement. In certain places, elevators operated by counterweights transported goods between levels.

That morning, sections of the upper canopy had been drawn aside by a system of ropes and pulleys, allowing sunlight to reach the walkways.

The smell of freshly baked bread mingled with the damp fragrance of the forest.

Merchants arranged their stalls. Children ran between houses. A blacksmith argued with a woman over the price of a knife that, according to her, had gone blunt after cutting only three branches.

“Because you tried to cut stone with it!” the blacksmith protested.

“It was a root!”

“A petrified root!”

Baranion passed them without interrupting the argument.

He liked this part of the morning.

There was something comforting about the way the settlement came to life, as though nothing existed beyond the trees.

But he knew better.

Beyond Fyor's hidden borders lay kingdoms, empires, enormous cities, and peoples who might never have heard of his home.

His father knew some of those lands.

Baranion wanted to know them too.

“You're late.”

The voice came from behind him.

Baranion turned and found Lyra Vaelis leaning against the railing of a bridge.

Her copper-colored hair was tied in a braid, and she carried a bow across her back. A small leather pouch hung from her belt.

“I'm not late.”

“Training started fifteen minutes ago.”

“That's a matter of interpretation.”

“Fifteen minutes?”

“Of the concept of punctuality.”

Lyra raised an eyebrow.

“Your father's waiting.”

Baranion grimaced.

“How angry does he look?”

“He's smiling.”

“That's worse.”

Lyra nodded solemnly.

“Much worse.”

The training ground stood on a circular platform built around a gigantic tree.

Dozens of young people practiced with wooden swords, bows, and staffs. Some trained alongside magical beasts.

A girl ran between obstacles while a small blue-furred fox leaped from platform to platform ahead of her.

Farther away, a boy tried to persuade a six-legged feline creature to obey a command.

The animal yawned.

The boy repeated the order.

The feline lay down.

Baranion smiled.

“At least someone's having a worse day than I am.”

“Baranion.”

Elrohir's voice wiped the smile from his face.

His father waited at the center of the platform.

He wore dark training clothes and held a wooden sword in his right hand.

Elrohir was tall, with black hair tied behind his head. A faint scar crossed his left eyebrow.

He didn't look particularly angry.

Which, as Lyra had pointed out, was worrying.

“Sleep well?” he asked.

“Very well.”

“Good. Vhar told me you needed your rest.”

Baranion narrowed his eyes.

“Vhar can't talk.”

“Not to you.”

A few nearby trainees laughed.

Elrohir tossed his son a wooden sword.

“Let's begin.”

Baranion caught it.

“No warm-up?”

“You've already had fifteen minutes to warm up.”

“I was on my way here.”

“Then you're warmed up.”

Lyra stepped aside, smiling.

Baranion took his stance.

His father did the same.

For a moment, neither moved.

Then Baranion lunged.

His first strike was fast, aimed at the left shoulder.

Elrohir dodged with the slightest movement.

The second strike came from below.

It was blocked.

Baranion pivoted on his foot, attempting to strike his father's side.

Elrohir's wooden sword touched his ribs.

“Dead.”

“I was still attacking!”

“Dead while attacking.”

Baranion stepped back, irritated.

“Again.”

Elrohir nodded.

This time, the boy attempted a more elaborate combination.

Two high strikes, a short retreat, and a thrust.

Elrohir avoided the thrust and placed the tip of his wooden sword against his son's throat.

“Dead again.”

“You could at least pretend I'm giving you some trouble.”

“I could. But that would make for terrible training.”

Baranion lowered his weapon.

“I'm never going to beat you.”

Elrohir lowered his sword as well.

“Not with that attitude.”

“You know what I mean.”

The man studied his son for a few seconds.

“You're thinking about the ritual.”

Baranion looked away.

Only a few weeks remained.

The Companion Ritual.

Ever since he was little, he had heard stories about the ceremony.

Some young people formed bonds with small, unassuming creatures. Others found companions who grew alongside them for decades.

The guardians often favored Umbral Ravens.

Those birds possessed extraordinary senses, remarkable intelligence, and a natural affinity for shadows.

Elrohir and Vhar were known for patrolling regions where other guardians could scarcely move without being noticed.

Baranion wanted a companion like Vhar.

Although preferably one that didn't throw stones at his head.

“Do you think I'll manage it?” he asked.

Elrohir seemed to consider the question.

“Manage what?”

“Bonding with an Umbral Raven.”

“Perhaps.”

“Just perhaps?”

“The ritual isn't a marketplace, Baranion. You don't choose a creature the way you choose a sword.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

The boy remained silent.

Elrohir rested the sword on his shoulder.

“A companion doesn't exist to fulfill your dreams. It has its own instincts, desires, and fears. A bond requires more than strength.”

“I know, Father.”

“Then stop thinking only about what you want to receive.”

Baranion frowned.

“And think about what I have to offer?”

Elrohir smiled.

“Now you're beginning to understand.”

A croak came from above.

Vhar was perched on a branch, watching them.

Baranion pointed at him.

“I'd offer him the chance to learn some manners.”

The raven dropped another stone.

Elrohir had to turn his face away to hide his laughter.

That afternoon, Baranion was given a simple task.

He had to deliver a small bundle of medicinal herbs to one of the caretakers responsible for injured animals in the lower settlement.

It was hardly exciting work.

Especially for someone who dreamed of becoming a guardian.

“You could show a little more enthusiasm,” Lyra remarked as she walked beside him.

“I'm carrying herbs.”

“Important herbs.”

“Herbs that anyone could carry.”

“And yet they chose you.”

“Exactly.”

Lyra laughed.

The two descended a series of platforms until they reached an area where the trees grew closer together.

Little sunlight reached the ground there.

Most homes were located on the upper levels, but a few smaller buildings had been constructed near the forest floor.

The lower forest was dangerous.

Wild creatures roamed among the roots, and some regions remained unexplored even by the most experienced guardians.

Baranion knew the rules.

Never leave the marked paths.

Never cross the boundary stones.

Never follow unfamiliar animals.

And, most importantly, never approach areas where the concealment matrices became unstable.

Simple rules.

Rules he intended to obey.

At least that afternoon.

“Did you hear that?” Lyra asked.

Baranion stopped.

“Hear what?”

A low sound drifted through the forest.

Something between a croak and a whimper.

Lyra reached for her bow.

The sound came again.

Baranion looked to the left.

Between two enormous roots lay a narrow passage covered in ferns.

“Sounds like a bird,” he said.

“Then it's probably a bird.”

“An injured one.”

“Probably.”

Baranion took a step.

Lyra grabbed his arm.

“No.”

“You don't even know what I'm going to do.”

“I know exactly what you're going to do.”

“It could be dying.”

“And it could be trying to lure something into a trap.”

Baranion looked toward the passage.

The sound came a third time.

Weaker.

He took a deep breath.

“I'm just going to look.”

Lyra closed her eyes.

“Those are the four words that always come before a disaster.”

The passage was narrow.

Roots formed a natural corridor, and the ground was covered with damp leaves.

Baranion moved slowly, pushing branches aside with one hand.

Lyra followed close behind, muttering about how she would have to explain everything to his father.

The sound stopped.

For a moment, Baranion thought the creature had escaped.

Then he saw the feathers.

Black.

Scattered across a moss-covered stone.

Farther ahead, beside the trunk of a fallen tree, lay a raven.

It was smaller than Vhar, but larger than any ordinary bird.

One of its wings was twisted at an unnatural angle.

Dried blood stained its feathers.

The animal lay motionless.

“Is it alive?” Lyra whispered.

Baranion approached.

The raven opened its eyes.

And the boy stopped.

They were gray.

Not the dull gray of stone, but a silvery shade that seemed to reflect a light that wasn't there.

The bird watched Baranion without making a sound.

“Easy,” he murmured. “I'm not going to hurt you.”

He extended his hand.

The raven did not retreat.

Baranion found that strange.

Even injured creatures usually tried to flee when someone approached.

But this bird simply watched him.

As though it had been waiting.

He knelt.

That was when he noticed.

Sunlight streamed through a gap in the canopy, illuminating the stone, the fallen trunk, and part of the ground.

The leaves cast shadows.

The roots cast shadows.

Even the small feathers scattered across the ground cast shadows.

But the raven did not.

Baranion blinked.

He shifted his head to look from another angle.

Nothing.

The sunlight fell directly upon the creature's body.

And yet there was no shadow beneath it.

“Lyra.”

“What?”

“Look at the ground.”

The girl frowned.

“What about it?”

“Its shadow.”

Lyra followed his gaze.

The silence that followed seemed to make the entire forest grow quieter.

“That's not normal,” she said.

“I noticed.”

“Baranion…”

The raven slowly raised its head.

Its silver eyes fixed upon the boy.

Then something even stranger happened.

The bird tilted its neck and gave a short croak.

Baranion shivered.

Not because of the sound.

But because, for an instant, he had the impression that the raven had tried to speak his name.

Bringing the creature back proved more difficult than Baranion had imagined.

First, because the raven didn't seem to trust Lyra.

Second, because its injured wing made every movement painful.

And third, because Lyra spent the entire journey reminding him that this was a terrible idea.

“Your father will find out.”

“He won't.”

“Vhar will find out.”

Baranion hesitated.

“Vhar doesn't have to find out.”

“You do know he can sense things through his bond with your father, don't you?”

“You're exaggerating.”

“Am I?”

Baranion looked at the raven wrapped in his cloak.

The bird remained still.

Too still.

“We could take it to the caretaker,” Lyra suggested.

“And tell her where we found it?”

“Yes.”

“She'll call the guardians.”

“Probably.”

“And they might take the raven away.”

Lyra sighed.

“You don't know that.”

Baranion carefully tightened his hold on the cloak.

“I'm not abandoning it.”

The girl studied her friend for a few seconds.

Then she shook her head.

“One day, your stubbornness is going to be the death of me.”

“So you'll help?”

“I'll help the raven. You're a secondary problem.”

Baranion hid the bird in a small storage room beside his bedroom.

It was where he kept old equipment, books, ropes, and objects he found during his walks.

Lyra brought clean water and some bandages.

Together, they tended to the injured wing as best they could.

The raven made no attempt to attack them.

It merely watched.

When Lyra finished securing the final bandage, she stepped back.

“That should help until we find someone who actually knows how to treat its wing.”

Baranion nodded.

“Thank you.”

She crossed her arms.

“I still think we should tell someone.”

“Tomorrow.”

“You said that about the vase you broke at my mother's house.”

“It was an accident.”

“Three years ago.”

“An old accident.”

Lyra rolled her eyes.

Before leaving, however, she looked at the raven again.

The bird was perched on a crate.

Its silver eyes followed her every movement.

“Baranion…”

“Yes?”

“I don't know what you found in that forest.”

The boy said nothing.

“But be careful.”

When Lyra left, Baranion closed the door.

The evening sunlight streamed through the storage room's small window.

He sat down in front of the creature.

“So… you need a name.”

The raven tilted its head.

“I can't keep calling you Raven.”

The bird blinked.

“How about Ash?”

No reaction.

“Silver?”

The raven turned its head away.

“Don't like it?”

Silence.

Baranion sighed.

“You're just as difficult as Vhar.”

The bird gave a low croak.

The boy smiled.

“All right. We'll think of something tomorrow.”

He stood.

That was when he noticed something.

On the wall behind the raven, illuminated by the setting sun, was the crate's shadow.

The window's shadow.

Baranion's shadow.

But not the bird's.

He stared for a few moments.

Then he approached the window and partially closed the curtain.

The light changed.

The shadows lengthened.

Still, the raven cast none.

Baranion felt that same chill again.

“What happened to you?” he whispered.

The bird did not answer.

But its silver eyes seemed more attentive than before.

That night, Elrohir returned late from patrol.

Baranion was sitting at the table when his father entered.

“How was your day?” Elrohir asked, removing his cloak.

“Normal.”

The man looked at him.

“Normal?”

“Very normal.”

“Interesting.”

Elrohir placed his sword on a stand.

“Was Lyra here?”

Baranion felt his stomach tighten.

“She was.”

“And you went down to the lower levels?”

“We delivered the herbs.”

“Is that all?”

Baranion took a moment to answer.

“That's all.”

Elrohir studied his son.

For a moment, Baranion was certain his father knew.

Then the man sighed.

“Some of the matrices near the western border showed disturbances today.”

Baranion looked up.

“Disturbances?”

“Minor failures. Nothing that puts the settlement in immediate danger, but those responsible are investigating.”

“What caused them?”

“We don't know yet.”

Elrohir sat down.

“For now, I want you to stay away from the lower regions unless you're accompanied.”

Baranion nodded.

“All right.”

His father seemed satisfied.

“And go to bed early. We'll continue training tomorrow.”

“Okay.”

Baranion stood.

But before he reached the stairs, Elrohir spoke again.

“Son.”

He stopped.

“Yes?”

“If you find anything strange in the forest, come and talk to me.”

Baranion felt his face grow warm.

“I know.”

Elrohir nodded.

“Good night.”

“Good night, Father.”

The storage room was silent when Baranion entered.

The raven slept on the crate, its head tucked beneath its feathers.

The boy checked the water and adjusted the small blanket he had placed beneath the bird.

Then he sat on the floor.

“My father said the matrices are having problems.”

The raven didn't move.

“You wouldn't have anything to do with that, would you?”

Silence.

Baranion rested his head against the wall.

“Of course not. You're just an injured bird.”

Outside, the wind stirred the branches.

The moon appeared between the leaves.

The boy spent several minutes watching the raven.

Then he rose to return to his bedroom.

At that moment, the lamp on the table flickered.

Baranion stopped.

There was no wind inside the storage room.

The flame shrank.

Then returned to normal.

He looked at the raven.

The bird was awake.

Its silver eyes shone in the dim light.

“What is it?”

No answer.

The lamp flickered again.

Baranion caught movement from the corner of his eye.

He turned toward the wall.

His own shadow was cast across the wood.

But something was wrong.

He stood perfectly still.

His shadow did not.

Slowly, the dark silhouette raised an arm.

Baranion felt his blood run cold.

His real arm remained motionless.

On the wall, the shadow reached toward the raven.

The bird gave a hoarse croak.

The lamp went out.

Darkness swallowed the storage room.

Baranion stumbled backward, tripping over a crate.

“Who's there?”

No answer.

For an instant, he heard only his own breathing.

Then, in the darkness, the raven's silver eyes opened.

And a voice emerged.

Low.

Distant.

Like a whisper rising from far beneath the forest's roots.

“Find me.”

Baranion froze.

The voice had not come from the bird.

Nor from the window.

Nor from anywhere he could identify.

It seemed to have risen from within the darkness itself.

Outside, far beyond the suspended houses, an alarm bell began to ring.

Once.

Twice.

Three times.

The signal that something had breached the settlement's defenses.

And for the first time in his life, Baranion was afraid of his own shadow.

End of Chapter I — The Shadowless Bird
